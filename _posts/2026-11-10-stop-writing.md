In this essay, I put forward some ideas on what the future of
mathematical culture could look like as well as thoughts and concrete
suggestions for how we get there. My focus is centred around a more
diverse ecosystem of mathematical output, moving away from traditional
practices of writing and submitting papers to journals. My vision is
aspirational, formed through a fair amount of dreaming, as well as
insights from others. Through my research, it became clear to me that as
a community we have vastly different views on how we approach the use of
AI in maths as well as its current and predicted capabilities. As much
as possible, I have tried to envisage a robust community that can adapt
to developments in AI whilst making space for people to contribute in
the way they feel most comfortable.

## Stop writing papers, really?!
My main aim is to provide some direction and cultivate positivity around
the future of mathematics. Before that though, let me address what I
believe we should *not* do. The title of this essay is purposefully
incendiary and stems from a deep feeling that we cannot simply try to do
business as usual. The pressure to publish has been the prominent
driving force in most mathematicians' careers, often drowning out the
rest of what we do. Arguably the community was long overdue some
introspection but the advent of AI gives us no choice.

The situation requires a monumental shift in how we approach mathematics
as well as a complete reform of our value system, rather than clunky
attempts to try and accommodate new advances into the status quo. (I
realise that this view is not held by everyone, but it is beyond the
scope of this essay to convince people of this[^1]). This is why we
urgently need to stop writing papers and focus on other activities.

In reality, I know that this is idealistic and that there are many
obstacles to change. Our institutions are built around the current
system and will be slow to adjust, a problem that disproportionately
affects junior academics and especially students. I am a post-doc and am
acutely aware that my publication list will remain the most influential
factor in obtaining a permanent job and grants in the coming years. If I
do get tenure at my current university, as I hope I will, my pay is
affected by how many papers I write.

There is another issue which is simply that this is what we know. I
myself will contradict my own advice. I have a pile of unfinished
projects that I plan to complete for a variety of reasons, some because
I am invested, some because I feel that I owe it to younger colleagues
who will benefit from the experience and just one that I believe
provides new ideas that cannot (currently) be obtained by pushing a
button. Nonetheless I believe I am not alone in at times feeling quite
lost[^2] over the last months and I hope that my suggestions can provide
some purpose moving forward.

## Ok, so let's at least write fewer papers. What should we do instead?

Below I lay out different axes of activities (in no particular order),
briefly explaining how we currently approach them and some ideas of what
we can do differently. All of them are broadly aimed at advancing and
facilitating human understanding of mathematics.

A.  **Atlasing:** In neuroscience[^3], *atlasing* refers to creating
    models of the brain that provide a standardised framework for many
    individual brain *maps*. I like this as a metaphor for the process
    of collating and canonising different mathematical works. This has
    typically been done through books and surveys, which remain
    incredibly valuable resources. One particularly ambitious example of
    this was [The Princeton Companion to
    Mathematics](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691118802/the-princeton-companion-to-mathematics?srsltid=AU7gw4WCa1MhONrF6LdjnkMdxAM9nWF4yHvSyitPxZm9YZn3Ha4KiJ-9)
    which aimed to overview all of modern mathematics. Moving forward we
    should be more systematic and dynamic in our efforts to atlas. I
    don't think it is so crazy to aim for a comprehensive online
    mathematical encyclopaedia that catalogues topics, theorems,
    concepts, proof methods and their interconnections and which is
    continually being expanded and refined. On the formal level, this is
    already happening[^4].

B.  **Big Mathematics:** This is a term used by Tao to mean[^5]
    'large-scale decentralised collaboration between humans and
    machines'. I would like to slightly broaden this definition by not
    imposing the use of machines. Indeed, I see the crowdsourcing
    [Polymath](https://polymathprojects.org/) project as an innovative
    example of big mathematics. Once we move away from a system that
    lauds individual ownership of results, we can become more open and
    collaborative on a large scale. I would love to see more accounts of
    failed attempts or half-proven results, as well as logs of what
    *cannot* be currently achieved by AI. In this way, I think we will
    save a lot of duplication by people working in secrecy.

C.  **Curation:** By definition, to curate is to select, organise and
    present. Whilst similar to **A**, I would contrast them by placing
    **A** as an attempt to create permanence, whilst **C** is concerned
    with processing the torrent of brand-new mathematics. Good curation
    picks out results/progress, places it in a wider context and tells
    you why you should care about it. This is a role that has been
    underserved with the exceptions I know of being some blogs[^6],
    [Quanta](https://www.quantamagazine.org/mathematics/) magazine and
    the [Bourbaki
    seminar](https://www.bourbaki.fr/seminaires/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
    (and to some extent
    [MathReviews](https://www.ams.org/publications/math-reviews/math-reviews)
    and workshops on current trends). With more maths being generated
    and the fall of many guiding conjectures, we will need more
    curation. This can take the form of podcasts, blogs, magazines and
    newsletters both for maths as a whole and for specialists in its
    many corners.

D.  **Dialogue:** Mathematicians talk to each other all the time,
    whether sharing ideas at a blackboard, debating our culture over
    coffee or catching up at conferences. These ongoing conversations
    are deeply useful but are mostly localised. Our global community
    also needs spaces to discuss and debate more widely and they are
    lacking[^7]. Comments sections on blogs such as Bloom's [Erdős
    problem website](https://www.erdosproblems.com/) have almost
    accidently become forums. We should create avenues for meaningful
    conversations, both about maths itself and about the culture. For
    example, a repository where people can share feedback, questions,
    answers and comments on papers/preprints would surely be useful.
    Maybe even a mathematics focused social media or a shared discord or
    slack. I don't mean that we should move completely online. On the
    contrary, I think we should also create more seminars, reading
    groups and round-table discussions. There is space for both to
    flourish.

E.  **Exposition:** This one has been grabbing a lot of attention[^8]
    due to AI's current inability to present ideas well. If we're honest
    with ourselves, we were also lacking in this as humans. I know
    plenty of highly influential papers that are terribly written and a
    bunch more that contain some beautiful idea that is completely
    obscured by the technicalities needed to get the proof over the
    line. The situation with talks is even worse, with the plenary
    lectures at our biggest conferences essentially being awards for
    research and with no expectation on the speaker or from the audience
    to understand after 5 minutes in. On the flip side, there are
    certainly some amazing storytellers in our community and reading
    their work or seeing them speak has made me realise what an art it
    is. It is exciting to see what we can achieve in terms of exposition
    when we give it the real weight and respect it deserves. It can and
    should come in many different forms and for the full spectrum of
    audiences, from experts, to students and researchers in other areas,
    to the general public.

## Ok but what should we actually do now?

The areas outlined above are not new but need much more attention and,
most importantly, the development of platforms and infrastructure to
highlight and reward them. This is the time to innovate and it is
happening already[^9]. [Proofs and
Prompts](https://proofsandprompts.com/), for example, is a great example
facilitating **D** and the new video journal [Mathematical
Discourse](https://www.mathematicaldiscourse.org/) feels like an
important move towards developing **E**. Further initiatives include the
[Mathathon](https://mathathonchallenge.com/) coding project to develop
new proofs and expositions of known results (**B**,**E**), the
open-source mathematical formalisation project
[Mathlib](http://mathlib-initiative.org/) (**A**,**B**) and
[Transformation](https://www.quantamagazine.org/updates/transformation/),
a new weekly digest from Quanta tracking news in maths and AI (**C**).

I hope to see many more exciting projects come to fruition and I believe
we all have a part to play in revolutionising what modern mathematics
looks like. In the past, we have placed a great deal of responsibility
on relatively few, those that have established themselves to the extent
that they can take time away from proving theorems to serve as editors,
give colloquia and write books. These people have an important role to
play in providing leadership, expertise and stature to new initiatives
but we cannot rely solely on them to build everything, there is simply
too much to do. Moreover, we should take this opportunity to diversify
the voices at the table. In particular, young researchers as well as
groups that are underrepresented in our current community will bring
vital new perspectives to the conversation. I encourage everyone to
think about how the future can look and how they want to contribute to
it. Apply for grants to help realise your ideas and support and
contribute to the ideas of others.

We should think with longevity and establish long-term alternatives to
traditional journals. We can borrow from what worked before and aim to
incentivise contribution by creating venues for other types of
mathematical output that are competitive and prestigious. However, we
should also explore new ways of rewarding output. We can look at
measuring forms of engagement from the community that go beyond
citations, maybe through views or voting systems (as used by [Math
Overflow](https://mathoverflow.net/tour), for example). Learning also
from our mistakes in the past, we should avoid outsourcing ownership of
our infrastructure and should celebrate community-led alternatives.

It is incredibly difficult to predict the future of maths but for now,
developing along axes **A**-**E** represents a tremendous workload of
things that we can be focusing on, all of which I can only see as being
beneficial.
